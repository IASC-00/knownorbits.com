Geminids 2026: when and where to watch

Watch the Geminids on Sunday night, December 13, 2026, into Monday morning, December 14. Look east after about 9 PM. The radiant in Gemini climbs through the evening and is almost overhead by 2 AM, and the thin crescent Moon sets around 8:50 PM, so the rest of the night is dark.

Times on this page are for Philadelphia, in Eastern time. The plan works across the US.

The peak, and why Sunday night

The International Meteor Organization puts the 2026 maximum at 14h UT on Monday, December 14. That's 9 AM Eastern, in daylight for North America. The peak is broad, though. The zenithal rate (below) stays above 100 for roughly 10 to 12 hours, and the well-observed maxima of the past two decades fall between 4 PM Eastern on December 13 and 1 PM on December 14.

So the dark hours that count are Sunday night into Monday morning.

Where to look, hour by hour

Geminids seem to fly out of one spot in Gemini, the radiant. The higher it is, the more meteors you see. Here's how it climbs over Philadelphia on the night of December 13 to 14. We computed these from the radiant's published position.

You don't have to stare at the radiant. The meteors fly away from it across the whole sky, and the ones near it look short, because you see them nearly end-on.

The Moon stays out of the way

New Moon is December 9. On the peak night the Moon is a waxing crescent, about 20% lit, and it sets at 8:49 PM in Philadelphia. The next night it sets at 9:51 PM, 26% lit. Either way you get a dark sky for the best hours.

How many you'll really see

The 150 an hour is the zenithal hourly rate: what one person would count under a perfect dark sky with the radiant straight overhead. Real counts always come in lower. NASA's Geminids page says to expect 40 to 50 an hour under dark skies, and from a city or suburb you'll see fewer still. Get away from streetlights if you can.

Geminids hit the air at about 35 km/s.

They come from an asteroid

Most meteor showers come from comets. The Geminids come from 3200 Phaethon, a rocky asteroid found in 1983. It circles the Sun every 524 days on a stretched orbit (eccentricity 0.89) that dips to 0.14 AU from the Sun, far inside Mercury's 0.39 AU.
