Winter solstice 2026: the date, the time and Stonehenge's sky

The winter solstice is Monday, December 21, 2026, at 20:50 UT, which is 3:50 PM Eastern and 12:50 PM Pacific. That evening at Stonehenge the Sun sets at 4:01 PM local time, about 1° farther north along the horizon than it did when the great stones went up around 2500 BC.

The stones haven't moved. The sky has. Here's what changed, worked out for the site.

The midwinter sunset has moved

Stand at the center of Stonehenge and look southwest along the stones' axis. At midwinter the Sun sets in the gap between the two tallest trilithons. On a horizon 0.5° high, the last gleam of the Sun was at 230.0° around 2500 BC. In 2026 it's at 231.0°, about two Sun widths to the right.

The reason is Earth's tilt, which was 23.97° then and is 23.44° now. In the Sun's height the change is only about one Sun width, which is how archaeoastronomers usually quote it. At Stonehenge's latitude the Sun meets the horizon at a shallow angle, so that small change in height becomes a bigger sideways step.

Our 2026 sunset numbers match the US Naval Observatory to the minute and round to the same whole degrees as timeanddate.

The pole star was Thuban, not Polaris

Earth's axis slowly wobbles, one lap in about 26,000 years, so the point the sky turns around drifts across the stars. Around 2500 BC that point sat 1.7° from Thuban, a star in Draco, while Polaris circled 25° away. Today Polaris is 0.6° from the pole.

Thuban came closest, within 0.1°, around 2800 BC. Polaris will be closest around AD 2100. Thuban was never a bright beacon, just the closest star, at magnitude 3.7. The next bright star near the pole is Vega, around AD 13,600, and it never gets closer than about 6°.

Arcturus never set

Around 2500 BC, Arcturus, one of the brightest stars in the sky, never set at Stonehenge. On late-summer nights it skimmed 7° above the north horizon around midnight, then climbed again. Today the same star sets in the northwest.

Two things changed. Earth's axis wobbled, and Arcturus itself moves fast across the sky, 2.8° in 4,500 years. Without its own motion it would have dipped to 4.5° instead of 7°. Our star positions for 2500 BC match the published values for all ten stars we checked.

Same sky, other direction: back then Alpha Centauri cleared Stonehenge's southern horizon by only about 1° at its highest, so low that whether anyone saw it is an open question.
